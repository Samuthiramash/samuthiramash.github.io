AI and the Changing Appeal of Bug Bounty Programmes

AI is speeding up vulnerability discovery and report writing. The challenge is making sure faster findings are accurate, actionable, and worth acting on.

By Ashok Kumar Samuthira · 5 min read

Vulnerabilities can serve as entry points for threat actors. To identify weaknesses before they can be exploited, organisations often invite external security researchers to test their systems and report vulnerabilities responsibly. Through bug bounty programmes, researchers may be rewarded financially for their contributions, while organisations gain external perspectives, specialised skills, and expertise that internal teams may not always have.

As artificial intelligence reshapes cybersecurity, however, bug bounty programmes face new questions. Some observers have raised concerns about the appeal and sustainability of these programmes. What might be driving the change?

Discovery and reporting are getting faster

AI-powered tools can help researchers identify potential vulnerabilities more quickly, reducing the effort required for some types of security testing. Tasks that once demanded extensive manual analysis can increasingly be supported by automation.

AI can also help researchers prepare reports. It can organise findings, explain technical details, and improve the clarity of submissions, making it easier to produce a report in less time.

Validation remains the hard part

Faster discovery does not automatically mean better results. AI-assisted findings still need to be validated. Some may be false positives, lack sufficient evidence, or fail to demonstrate a meaningful security impact. When submission volumes rise, security teams may need to spend more time reviewing, reproducing, and prioritising reported issues.

This creates a potential imbalance: finding and documenting possible vulnerabilities can become faster, while confirming their validity and significance still requires careful judgement, technical expertise, and human oversight.

Adapting the bug bounty model

The impact of AI is not simply that it makes bug bounty programmes less appealing. Rather, it is changing the economics and expectations of vulnerability research. Researchers, programme operators, and internal security teams must adapt to a landscape where speed is increasingly accessible, but quality and trust remain essential.

AI can accelerate security work, but it does not remove the need for expertise. The real challenge is combining AI-driven efficiency with human judgement so genuine vulnerabilities are identified, validated, and addressed effectively.

As cybersecurity continues to evolve, bug bounty programmes will need to evolve with it. Their value may depend less on the number of reports submitted and more on findings that are accurate, actionable, and valuable.
